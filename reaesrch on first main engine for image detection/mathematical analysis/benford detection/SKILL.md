# Benford's Law Forgery Detection

## Purpose

Benford's Law states that in many naturally occurring numerical datasets, the leading (most significant) digit d ∈ {1,...,9} follows P(d) = log10(1 + 1/d) rather than a uniform distribution — roughly 30% of values start with 1, less than 5% start with 9. Natural-image transform coefficients (block DCT coefficients under JPEG compression, DWT subband coefficients under JPEG2000) inherit this property because their underlying statistics are well modeled by Laplacian/generalized-Gaussian distributions, which are known to produce Benford-conforming leading-digit statistics. Any operation that disturbs the original coefficient-generation process — re-quantization from a second JPEG/JPEG2000 compression, cut-and-paste splicing that mixes coefficient populations from different sources, contrast enhancement/gamma correction, histogram manipulation, or synthetic (GAN/CGI) generation that does not follow a real camera + lossy-compression pipeline — measurably distorts the leading-digit histogram away from the ideal logarithmic curve. Because the ideal curve is analytically known, the deviation between an image's empirical digit histogram and the Benford (or generalized Benford) curve can be turned into a compact, cheap, statistically-grounded tampering/synthesis score, making it a good lightweight first-pass or fusion-layer feature alongside heavier CNN-based forensic detectors.

## Techniques found in the literature

**1. Shan, M. (2014). "Research of Computer Forensics Based on Benford's Law."** *(shan2014.pdf, Advanced Materials Research Vols 989-994)*
Not image-specific — applied to file-size statistics in computer forensics, but establishes the baseline classical first-digit law P(d)=log10(1+1/d) and shows a table of higher-order digit distributions (2nd/3rd/4th digit). Deviation is assessed only visually/by comparing "Actual %" vs "Benford %" per digit bucket (no formal statistic). Included as the canonical exposition of Benford's Law and higher-digit tables that other papers build on. No chi-square/KL formalism, no image domain — background reference only.

**2. Thai, T.H., Retraint, F., Cogranne, R. (2012). "Statistical Model of Natural Images."** *(thai2012.pdf, ICIP 2012)*
Not a Benford paper per se, but foundational: derives a rigorous doubly-stochastic statistical model of pre-quantization DCT coefficients across the full acquisition chain (RAW Poisson-Gaussian noise → demosaicking → white balance → gamma correction → DCT). Models block variance as a Gamma distribution and DCT coefficients as a Gamma-mixture (generalizing Laplacian/Gaussian, verified by chi-square goodness-of-fit against a generalized-Gamma alternative on the Dresden Image Database). Relevant because it explains *why* DCT coefficients are Laplacian-like — the statistical prerequisite for Benford conformance — and gives the mathematical basis (Laplacian pdf) used by other papers to justify or derive the generalized Benford model.

**3. Moin, S.S., Islam, S. (2017). "Benford's Law for Detecting Contrast Enhancement."** *(moin2017.pdf, ICIIP 2017)*
Domain: DCT coefficients of JPEG-compressed images (grayscale, UCID dataset). Technique: extract first-significant-digit (FSD) histogram of DCT coefficients, compare to Benford's ideal curve using **chi-square divergence** χ² = Σ (p̂(d)-p(d))²/p(d)-style measure per image; mean χ² for unaltered images ≈0.0034–0.013 vs ≈0.005–0.079 for gamma-corrected (contrast-enhanced) images. The Benford-derived chi-square features (per digit, per DCT frequency) are fed into an **SVM classifier** (10-fold cross-validation) to detect (a) standard gamma-correction contrast enhancement and (b) contrast enhancement hidden behind an anti-forensic "local random dithering" attack that defeats histogram-gap-bin detectors (Cao et al.). Reported detection rates: ~93–99% Pd with 1.3–7.3% false-alarm rate across JPEG quality factors 50/70/90 and gamma values 0.5–2.0; SVM on Benford features remained robust to the anti-forensic attack where a competing gap-bin detector failed completely (0% detection).

**4. Singh, N., Bansal, R. (2015). "Analysis of Benford's Law in Digital Image Forensics."** *(singh2015.pdf, IEEE 2015)*
Domain: DCT coefficients of JPEG images AND DWT coefficients of JPEG2000 images. Technique: fit empirical FSD histogram to classic Benford curve p(x)=log10(1+1/x); shows single-compressed JPEG/JPEG2000 images closely follow Benford, but **double/multiple compression** (recompressing at a second, different quality factor) causes systematic deviation, quantified via a **deviation ratio = mean-square error between first-digit probability curves of original vs. double-compressed image**. Also shows copy-move (cut-paste) tampering does NOT visibly break the aggregate first-digit Benford curve (fails as a copy-move detector at 1st-digit resolution) but higher-order digits help. Extends the same MSE-deviation-ratio approach to DWT coefficients of JPEG2000 for detecting double compression and for glare-artifact detection (a spike appears at digit 5 in the DCT/DWT first-digit histogram of glare regions). No classifier — purely a deviation-metric/threshold approach.

**5. Wang, J., Cha, B-H., Cho, S-H., Kuo, C-C.J. (2009). "Understanding Benford's Law and Its Vulnerability in Image Forensics."** *(wang2009.pdf, ICME 2009)*
Domain: block DCT coefficients, theoretical/generalization paper. Derives the **generalized Benford's Law**: Bg(d) = α1·log10(α3 + 1/d^α2), fitting the FSD distribution produced when the underlying coefficient population is Laplacian or Gaussian rather than perfectly log-uniform (fitted parameters given for standard Laplacian: α1=1.05, α2=1.352, α3=1.061; standard Gaussian: α1=1.08, α2=2.55, α3=1.15). This generalized form is the correct model to fit to real DCT coefficient populations (better fit than the plain classical law). Critically, the paper also demonstrates the **vulnerability** of Benford-based detectors: a manipulation (e.g. double JPEG compression, dithering) that breaks Benford conformance can be masked by a subsequent *compensation* operation (histogram equalization, rescaling) that restores Benford conformance without undoing the tampering — i.e., Benford's law is not invariant/robust against deliberate anti-forensic counter-attacks.

**6. Bonettini, N., Bestagini, P., Milani, S., Tubaro, S. (2021). "On the Use of Benford's Law to Detect GAN-Generated Images."** *(bonettini2021.pdf, ICPR 2021)*
Domain: quantized block-DCT coefficients (generalizes the digit base b, not just base 10). Most complete/modern methodology: (1) partition image into 8×8 blocks, compute 2D-DCT, quantize per a JPEG quantization matrix at chosen quality factor; (2) compute the first-digit-in-base-b value db,n,Δ(k) for each block/frequency/quantization step; (3) form empirical pmf p̂(d); (4) fit generalized Benford's Law p(d)=β·logb(1+1/(γ+d^δ)) to the pmf via least-squares (analogous form to Wang et al.'s generalization but fit per-image); (5) compute **divergence between empirical pmf and fitted Benford curve** using three alternative measures — symmetrized KL (Jensen-Shannon) divergence, Rényi divergence, and Tsallis divergence; (6) concatenate divergence values across multiple digit bases b, DCT frequencies n, and JPEG quantization steps Δ into a feature vector (as small as 3 dims, up to ~540 dims); (7) feed feature vector to a **Random Forest classifier** (100 trees, Gini splitting) for natural-vs-GAN-generated binary classification. Achieves 99.83% average accuracy across 15 GAN/dataset combinations (CycleGAN, ProGAN), outperforming a fine-tuned Xception CNN baseline (89.64%) and a steganalysis-features+SVM baseline (91.03%), with far lower computational cost/training-data requirements. Notable limitation: accuracy collapses to near-random when the detector is trained on uncompressed images but tested against images subsequently JPEG-recompressed at unseen quality factors — retraining on compressed data recovers most (but not all) performance (>0.80 accuracy). Face-specific GAN images (StyleGAN2, StarGAN, GlowGAN) are harder to detect (72–97% depending on architecture) than generic scene GANs.

## Recommended approach for this engine

**Model choice:** Use the **generalized Benford's Law**, Bg(d) = β·log_b(1 + 1/(γ + d^δ)), not the plain classical log10(1+1/d) form. Real DCT/DWT coefficient populations are Laplacian/generalized-Gaussian, not perfectly log-uniform, so the classical law systematically under/over-predicts low digits; the generalized form (Wang 2009, refined by Bonettini 2021) fits the true coefficient statistics and gives a cleaner "clean/unmanipulated" baseline against which deviation is measured. Fit β, γ, δ per-image (or per fixed reference set) via least-squares against the empirical pmf, as in Bonettini et al. eq. (11).

**Transform domain:** Block **DCT (8×8, JPEG-aligned)** is the best-supported and most sensitive domain across the literature — it is used by 4 of 6 papers and gives the strongest, most reproducible signal because JPEG quantization is exactly the mechanism that imposes Benford conformance in the first place. DWT (JPEG2000) is a viable secondary domain (Singh 2015) if the engine needs to support JPEG2000/wavelet-coded inputs, using the same deviation-ratio logic at DWT level ~5. For DCT, do not use only the DC/lowest frequency — accuracy improves substantially by using multiple AC frequencies (first 5–9 in zig-zag order, per Bonettini's ablation) and multiple digit bases (b=10 alone is enough for most gains; combining a couple of bases gives marginal further improvement).

**Deviation metric:** Compute the empirical first-digit pmf p̂(d) per (frequency, quantization-step/quality-factor) combination, fit the generalized Benford curve p(d), then compute a **symmetrized divergence** — Jensen-Shannon divergence is the simplest and most stable (Rényi/Tsallis are viable alternatives but JS is well-behaved and simple to implement: D^JS = D^KL(p̂‖p) + D^KL(p‖p̂)). Avoid raw MSE-only "deviation ratio" (Singh 2015's method) as the sole metric — it works but is less standard and less directly comparable across images/quality factors than a proper information-theoretic divergence.

**Turning it into a per-image tampering score:** Follow Bonettini et al.'s feature-vector approach: for a small set of DCT frequencies (e.g., first 9 in zig-zag), a small set of digit bases (b=10, optionally b=20/40), and a few JPEG quality-factor-equivalent quantization steps, compute the divergence for each combination and concatenate into a feature vector (dimensionality can be kept small — even 3–50 features achieve >0.75–0.97 accuracy per Bonettini's Fig. 5). Feed this vector into a lightweight supervised classifier — Random Forest (Bonettini) or SVM (Moin) both work well and are cheap enough for a fusion-layer module; output the classifier's confidence/probability as the per-image Benford-based tampering/synthesis score, to be combined with other engine modules (noise, CFA, ELA, deep forgery detectors) in the fusion layer. For splicing/copy-move localization rather than whole-image classification, compute the divergence in a sliding-block/local-region fashion (per Singh's glare-detection example, where a spike at a specific digit flags the anomalous region) rather than globally.

**Known limitations to encode into the fusion logic (do not treat Benford score in isolation):**
- **Double/incompatible compression confound**: Any image that has been through more than one lossy JPEG compression cycle — even with no malicious editing — deviates from Benford's Law (Singh 2015, Wang 2009). This is a known *forensic signal* (indicates recompression) but also a **false-positive source** for tampering detection on legitimately-recompressed, resaved, or social-media-downloaded images. The engine should treat a Benford-deviation flag as "recompressed or possibly manipulated," not "definitely spliced," and cross-check with other cues.
- **Domain mismatch after resave**: The detector trained on uncompressed-domain features collapses to near-random accuracy when the test image is subsequently JPEG-recompressed at an unseen quality factor (Bonettini 2021). Train/calibrate the Benford model on images passed through the same JPEG pipeline (quality factor range) the engine expects to see in production, or explicitly bucket by estimated quality factor.
- **Anti-forensic vulnerability**: Benford conformance can be artificially restored by an attacker applying histogram equalization/dithering/rescaling after tampering (Wang 2009), or by anti-forensic contrast-mapping schemes designed to leave no telltale gap-bins (Moin 2017's evaluation of Cao et al.'s attack). The generalized-Benford+divergence+SVM/RF approach (Moin, Bonettini) is more robust to such attacks than raw histogram-gap-bin detectors, but should not be treated as unbeatable — use it as one signal among several.
- **First-digit resolution blindness to localized tampering**: Whole-image aggregate first-digit statistics are largely insensitive to small copy-move/splice regions (Singh 2015) — a copy-pasted patch from the *same* image or a similarly-compressed source will not shift the global first-digit histogram much. Use block-local/regional divergence maps (not just a single whole-image score) if splice localization is required, and rely on second/third-digit statistics or CNN-based localization for small-region tampering.
- **Natural high-compression / low-quality images**: Very low JPEG quality factors or heavily quantized images can show larger natural deviation from Benford even when unaltered (fewer effective coefficient values, coarser quantization bins), so score thresholds should be quality-factor-aware/calibrated rather than a single fixed cutoff.
