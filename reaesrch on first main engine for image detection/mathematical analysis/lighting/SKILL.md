# Lighting / Illumination Inconsistency Forgery Detection

## Purpose
When a face or object region is composited into a scene — whether by classical splicing or by GAN-based face swapping/reenactment — the inserted content rarely obeys the same physical light transport as the rest of the image. The direction and color of the dominant light source, the softness and position of cast/attached shadows, and the placement of specular highlights (in eyes, skin, teeth, jewelry) are all functions of the true 3D scene geometry and illuminant at capture time. A generator or a copy-paste operation typically does not solve for this consistently across the manipulated region and its surroundings, so illumination cues leave residual, often visually subtle, inconsistencies (e.g. shading that doesn't match the estimated light vector, highlight positions that disagree between two eyes, or hard edges at splice boundaries where local light statistics change abruptly). These cues are attractive for a forensic engine because they are largely orthogonal to compression/pixel-artifact-based detectors: a well-lit, high-quality deepfake can still defeat frequency or GAN-fingerprint detectors while failing a physically-grounded lighting-consistency check, making this a good complementary signal for fusion.

## Techniques found in the literature

**Title:** Exposing DeepFakes via Localizing the Manipulated Artifacts
**Authors:** Wenxin Li, Qi Wang, Run Wang, Lei Zhao, Lina Wang (Wuhan University), published in ICICS 2021 (LNCS 12919, Springer), DOI 10.1007/978-3-030-88052-1_1
**Source filename:** `Exposing-DeepFakes-via-Localizing-the-Manipulated-Artifacts.pdf`

**Important caveat on relevance:** despite the folder placement, this paper is **not** a photometric/illumination-modeling technique. It is a general-purpose learned artifact-localization method. It is included here because the authors motivate their approach in part by observing that different DeepFake generators leave different visible artifact signatures, and they explicitly call out that **FaceSwap-synthesized images exhibit "unnatural light around the eyes and nose"** as one of the observable artifact types their method is meant to help localize (Fig. 1, Section 1). No shading model, light-direction estimation, shadow/highlight geometry, or reflectance model is used anywhere in the paper — the "localization" is a learned attention map, not a physically interpretable lighting analysis.

**Technique / architecture:**
- A dual-attention module (channel attention + spatial attention, directly inspired by CBAM — Woo et al., ECCV 2018) is inserted into the feature maps of an existing DeepFake classifier backbone (evaluated on MesoNet, Meso-Inception, VGG-19, Xception, EfficientNet-B0).
- **Channel attention:** global average-pool and global max-pool the feature map `F ∈ R^(C×H×W)` into two C-length vectors, pass both through a shared bottleneck MLP (compression ratio 8), sum, and apply sigmoid to get a per-channel weight `M_c(F) = σ(MLP(GAP(F)) + MLP(GMP(F)))`. This tells the network which feature channels correspond to likely-artifact content (e.g. eye/mouth-related channels vs. background channels).
- **Spatial attention:** channel-pool (max + avg across channels) the feature map into two H×W maps, concatenate, and apply a 7×7 convolution + sigmoid to get a spatial weight map `M_s(F) = σ(f([F_avg^S, F_max^S]))`. This tells the network *where* in the image artifacts are concentrated.
- The two are applied sequentially (channel attention first, then spatial attention on the result) and multiplied elementwise into the feature map at each block of the backbone. For the first block, attention is applied right after the first convolution (finest-grained, used for localization); for later blocks it's applied after pooling.
- Crucially, this is trained with **only real/fake image-level labels** (no manipulation ground-truth masks needed), unlike the prior work it compares against (Dang et al., CVPR 2020, "FFD" — which needs supervised ground-truth masks). The spatial attention map from the first block is read out post-hoc as a pixel-level localization/heatmap of the artifact region.

**What it localizes and how:** artifact regions in general (blending boundaries, GAN checkerboard/texture artifacts, warping artifacts, and — per their own qualitative examples — lighting artifacts around eyes/nose for FaceSwap), not lighting inconsistency specifically. Localization quality is read directly off the learned spatial-attention map, upsampled to image resolution.

**Reported results:** Detection accuracy improved over unmodified backbones by 3.50% (MesoNet), 2.56% (Meso-Inception), 1.64% (VGG-19), 1.36% (Xception), 0.89% (EfficientNet), averaged across 6 datasets (FaceForensics++, Celeb-DF v2, UADFV, Deepfake-TIMIT, DeeperForensics, StyleGAN). AUC > 0.92 on all datasets except FF-LQ. Localization quality measured via PBCA (Pixel-wise Binary Classification Accuracy, > 0.70 on all datasets with ground truth) and IINC (Inverse Intersection Non-Containment, < 0.70), modestly outperforming the FFD baseline (PBCA 0.8315 vs 0.8289, IINC 0.6454 vs 0.6574 on the FaceApp comparison set) while producing visibly finer-grained maps (Fig. 7).

## Recommended approach for this engine

Because this single paper does not actually deliver a lighting/shading model, two complementary paths are recommended:

**1. Build an actual photometric lighting-consistency check (primary recommendation, not covered by the corpus yet):**
- Detect face region(s) via a landmark detector (e.g. dlib/MediaPipe) and fit a coarse 3D face model (3DMM, e.g. via a library like 3DDFA) to get surface normals per pixel in the face region.
- Estimate the illumination as low-order Spherical Harmonics (SH) coefficients per face using a standard Lambertian shading model: `I(n) = ρ * L · SH(n)`, solved via least squares between predicted shading (from normals) and observed pixel intensities in a diffuse (non-specular, non-shadowed) mask.
- For images/video with multiple faces or a face + background object, estimate SH lighting independently per region and compare the recovered light-direction vectors (or full SH coefficient vectors, compared via cosine similarity or L2 in the low-frequency SH space) between regions. A single physical scene should yield consistent illumination; a composited/swapped face will frequently disagree.
- Secondary signals to fold in: specular highlight position in each eye (should lie along the reflection of the same light source — compare highlight angle between left/right eye), and cast-shadow direction/softness at the jaw/neck boundary vs. estimated light direction.
- This mirrors established prior art in image forensics (e.g. Johnson & Farid's illuminant-direction inconsistency work, and O'Brien & Farid's specular-highlight consistency work) — worth sourcing those papers to fill this gap, since the current corpus folder does not contain a true photometric-model paper.

**2. Reuse the dual-attention artifact-localization network as a general (non-lighting-specific) artifact heatmap generator:**
- The CBAM-style dual-attention block is cheap to bolt onto any existing CNN classifier already used elsewhere in the pipeline (e.g. the frequency-domain or GAN-fingerprint classifier), and it comes "for free" with a pixel-level localization map without needing manipulation masks for training — only real/fake labels, which are much easier to obtain at scale.
- Use its output spatial-attention map (from the first block, before pooling) as one channel of the fusion-layer heatmap, alongside the photometric lighting-consistency map from approach (1). This gives the fusion layer both a general "where do artifacts concentrate" signal and a specific "is illumination physically consistent" signal.
- Do not rely on it alone for a lighting-specific claim — its attention is trained end-to-end on whatever artifact types the training data contains and will pick up compression/blending/texture cues as much as lighting cues; treat its heatmap as an unlabeled aggregate artifact score, not evidence specifically implicating illumination.

**Turning either into a tampering score for fusion:**
- Photometric path: define a per-region inconsistency score as `1 - cosine_similarity(SH_region_A, SH_region_B)` (or max pairwise disagreement across all detected face/object regions in the frame), normalized to [0,1], with a calibrated threshold from a validation set. For video, smooth/aggregate this per-frame score with a short temporal window since natural lighting is temporally coherent and a flickering inconsistency score is itself suspicious.
- Attention-network path: aggregate the spatial attention map's peak/mean activation within the face bounding box into a scalar score, and optionally return the upsampled map itself as a visual heatmap overlay for the fusion layer / UI.

**Known limitations:**
- This is the only paper in the `lighting` folder, and it is not actually a lighting-model paper — the corpus has thin/no coverage of true photometric illumination-consistency forensics (SH lighting estimation, specular highlight geometry, shadow-consistency methods). Recommend sourcing at least 2-3 more papers directly on this topic (e.g. classic illuminant-direction / specular-highlight forensics literature) before treating "lighting inconsistency" as a validated module rather than a placeholder.
- The dual-attention method's cross-dataset generalization is weak (Table 3 in the paper): accuracy drops substantially when trained on one dataset/GAN family and tested on another, because attention learns generator-specific artifact signatures rather than a general physical principle. A purely learned artifact map should not be presented as "lighting inconsistency detection" to downstream consumers of the fusion score.
- Photometric SH-lighting estimation from a single face crop is itself an approximation (Lambertian assumption, low-frequency SH ignores hard shadows/specular components) and is sensitive to face pose, occlusion, and low resolution — expect it to be a moderate-confidence signal, best combined with other cues rather than used standalone.
